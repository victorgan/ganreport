Nearly every pedigreed cat alive traces to one of a few founding populations, a handful of odd kittens whose mutations someone decided to keep, or a deliberate cross drawn up on paper. This atlas maps who came from whom, what that makes them look like, how they behave, and who each one is actually for.

01 · Taxonomy

Where breeds come from

Cat breeds are not species and they are not old. Most are younger than the aeroplane. The useful first cut is not by looks but by mechanism: was this population shaped by a place, by a single mutation someone chose to propagate, by a breeder combining existing breeds, or by crossing in a wild species? 04 · Frequency

How likely you are to meet one

There is no single ranking of cat popularity. Registries count only pedigreed kittens registered with them, and they disagree: the CFA's 2026 list put the Maine Coon first and the Ragdoll second, while breeds that dominate elsewhere — the Bengal, the Scottish Fold, the Munchkin — sit low or unrecognised. The index below blends registry counts across the major bodies with how often each breed actually turns up in homes. Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a leaderboard.

Every pedigreed cat put together is a rounding error next to the domestic shorthair — the unpedigreed moggy is roughly 95–97% of the world's owned cats. Nothing on this list is common in absolute terms.

06 · Matching

Which cat is for you

Answer honestly about the life you actually live, not the one you mean to. Breed is a weak predictor next to individual personality and early socialisation — but it does shift the odds on noise, activity, coat work and how badly the cat minds being alone.

On the numbers. Trait ratings are consensus breed-standard temperament, compressed to a 1–5 scale for comparison. They describe tendencies across a breed, not any individual cat; within-breed variation is larger than most between-breed differences. Weight, lifespan and health notes follow the major registries and veterinary breed-predisposition literature.

On hypoallergenic cats. There aren't any. The main allergen, Fel d 1, is in saliva and skin, not hair, so hairless breeds are not exempt. Siberians, Balinese and Russian Blues are often reported to produce less of it; the evidence is thin and varies more between individual cats than between breeds.

On the flagged breeds. Several popular breeds are defined by a mutation that also causes harm — folded ears come with cartilage disease in every Scottish Fold, flat faces with obstructed airways, taillessness with spinal defects. Those cards carry a welfare note. It is not an argument against the cats already alive, which deserve homes.
