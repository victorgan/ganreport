An atlas of trip archetypes

The shape
of a trip

Where you go matters less than the shape of how you go. One base or seven, a line or a loop, a place seen once or the same place for twenty years — that structure decides the pace, the cost, the tiredness and what you remember, long before the destination gets a say.

Start with the only question that matters: what do you want to be true when you get home?

Taxonomy

Six shapes, and everything is one of them

Sort trips by destination and you get a map. Sort them by structure and you get something more useful: an anchor and a hop can go to the same country and produce opposite weeks. Each archetype below is drawn as its own route — the number of circles is how many bases, their size is how long you stay, and the line between them is how you move.

Vocabulary

Eight dials set how a trip feels

The shape decides the structure; these decide the texture. Most disappointing trips are not badly chosen so much as badly set — a pace that suited nobody, a planning level that trapped you, comfort bought where access was needed. Learn to read a proposed trip as eight settings, and to say which one is wrong.

The central trade-off

Rest and story pull against each other

Almost every trip is sold as both restorative and transformative. Most are not: effort, unfamiliarity and early starts are what make a trip memorable, and they are the same things that stop it being restful. The dashed line marks a combined score of seven. Two kinds of trip get past it — the long ones, and the ones where somebody else is carrying the logistics — and nothing at all reaches ten.

Side by side

Two shapes, same fortnight

The instructive comparisons are between trips that could occupy exactly the same two weeks and the same budget: one city or four, a loop or a base, this year's new place or the one you have been to eleven times.

On the ratings. Every archetype is scored 0–5 on eight dials plus rest and story. They describe a typical good version of that shape as it is normally done — a cheap version of an expensive shape exists, and so does a punishing version of a gentle one. The numbers are for comparing shapes with each other, not for grading a specific trip.

On taste. There is no ranking here and no shape that is more sophisticated than another. The package holiday is extremely good at what it does, and the six-month overland is a bad answer to most questions. The only real error is choosing a shape whose costs you did not intend to pay.

On other people. Nearly every rule here bends for the people you travel with. A shape that suits you and nobody else in the party is the wrong shape, and the negotiation is best done over the dials, before anything is booked, rather than on day four.
