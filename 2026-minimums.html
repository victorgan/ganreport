The four tiers

Tiers are not ranked by importance. They're ranked by how fast a deficit becomes acute — how loudly it screams for attention. Bar width is time-to-failure on a log scale.

The dangerous tier is 2. Tier 0 and 1 deficits announce themselves. Tier 2 deficits erode silently over years and get misattributed to Tier 3 — riders with a loneliness deficit go looking for a purpose problem.

The group

23 riders, one group. You can't drop anyone — every one of these is yours and comes along. So the group always slows to the weakest, which is why the line sits where it does. Everything to the right of it is surplus: real capacity you hold and cannot spend. Tap any rider to set their pace.

Where this comes from — Liebig's barrel, and why this app isn't one

The rule underneath both pictures is the law of the minimum: growth is limited by the scarcest resource relative to need, not by the total of all resources available. Pour more nitrogen onto soil that's short of phosphorus and nothing happens. It was first proposed by Carl Sprengel in 1840 and later popularised by Justus von Liebig.

The famous illustration — a barrel with staves of unequal length, holding water only as high as the shortest one — came later, and wasn't Liebig's. It's credited to Dobenecks, who used it to explain Liebig's law. Liebig gave the rule; someone else drew the picture that made it stick.

Same function, different verb

barrel — a stock
paceline — a rate

Six identical values, two drawings. Both compute min, never the sum and never the average — which is the entire claim. The difference is what they measure. A barrel measures a stock: how much a container holds, settled and static. A paceline measures a rate: how fast a group sustains, recomputed continuously. For a life audit the rate framing is the more honest one, because you don't fill your life once and read off the level — the minimum re-runs every day.

Where the barrel quietly lies

The barrel assumes the short stave doesn't affect the others — it simply caps the total. Agronomists flag exactly this as the analogy's weak point: nutrients interact, and a deficiency in one degrades the uptake and efficiency of the rest, so the tidy assumption usually fails in practice.

The objection lands harder on a life. Financial scarcity, chronic pain and untreated depression don't merely set the level — they tax every other domain, which is the bandwidth cost this whole model turns on. A short stave never shortens its neighbours. A suffering rider genuinely does slow everyone else down. That's the one place the cycling picture isn't just a nicer fit for a phone screen, but a truer account of the failure mode.

The cooper

In formal treatments of the law, an adapting system behaves like a cooper: it spends its repair budget on whichever stave is shortest rather than spreading it evenly, because that's the only allocation that raises capacity. That is precisely what the focus card above is doing — and precisely what most people don't do, because the tall staves are the ones they're good at.

Relatives

Leontief's production function is the same rule in economics. Goldratt's Theory of Constraints is the same rule in operations. Different fields, one function.

History

Group pace over time. The only line worth tracking — average pace across all 23 riders is a vanity metric, because bringing up a rider who's already ahead changes nothing.

On using this. Paces are self-reported and the anchors are population-level heuristics with wide variance, not targets. Some riders can't be brought up — chronic illness, caregiving, bereavement, structural poverty can hold one down permanently through no failure of strategy. When that's the case this is describing a constraint, not a to-do list, and the right move is to build around it rather than keep assaulting it. One methodological note: these paces are ordered categories with written anchors, not a measured scale, so the app counts riders and takes minimums but never averages or sums them — the difference between a 4 and a 2 isn't ‘two points’ of anything. Nothing here is medical or financial advice. Data is stored privately to your account and never shared.
