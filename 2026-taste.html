A working atlas of flavour

How to taste

Taste is not a gift you are born with or without. It is a vocabulary, a set of things to notice, and the habit of asking what a dish is doing. Everything here is meant to be used at the table.

Start here: something tastes off. Which is it?

Pick a fault. Nearly every disappointing plate is one of these eight, and each has a specific fix — that is the whole trick.

The controls

Everything you taste is ten levers in some combination

Five of them are tastes proper, detected on the tongue. The rest are sensations the brain files under flavour anyway: fat, burn, smell, texture, temperature. A cook adjusts these; an eater learns to hear them separately. Learn to ask which lever is pulled hard, and which one is missing.

Taxonomy

Four forces make every flavour there is

Classifying food by country tells you where it was eaten, not what it tastes like. Classify instead by what was done to it. Flavour comes from time, from water, from fire, or from the hand — and the dishes people rate highest almost always stack two or three of those forces on one plate. The central tension

Richness has to be paid for

The oldest rule in cooking is that fat needs acid. Fat coats the tongue and dulls it; acid strips it clean and resets the palate for the next bite. Plot the two against each other and the food you keep going back to sits on the diagonal — every gain in richness matched by something sharp. The corners are where dishes fail, and each corner fails in its own recognisable way.

Vocabulary

Words that do work

Vague praise is the enemy of a developing palate: if the best you can say is "amazing", you cannot tell tomorrow what you liked. These are the terms worth owning, each with the case it actually fits and the near-neighbour people mix it up with. Use one precise word and the memory sticks.

Practice

A palate is trained, not issued

Three things move it: paying attention in a fixed order so nothing gets skipped, tasting things side by side so differences have something to be different from, and climbing gradually into flavours that are unpleasant at first and load-bearing later.

The order of attention

Run this on one dish per meal. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the entire difference between eating and tasting.

    Calibration drills

    Each one is a side-by-side comparison you can run at home for the price of a few ingredients. Tasting one thing teaches you almost nothing; tasting two things that differ in exactly one way teaches you that one thing permanently.

    At the table

    Most eating happens in restaurants, where you are choosing rather than cooking. These are the moves that turn a meal into information.

    Climbing into acquired tastes

    Bitterness, funk and burn are defensive reflexes — the body reading possible poison. Tolerance is built by repeated safe exposure, in order, which is why these are numbered. Each rung should stop being difficult before you step to the next.

    Side by side

    Two dishes, one difference

    The useful comparisons isolate a single variable: the same broth clear or emulsified (phở against tonkotsu), the same fish raw or cured (ōtoro against salt-cured anchovy), the same sugar sweet or burnt (gelato against crème brûlée).

    On the numbers. Every dish is scored 0–5 on eight axes. The scores describe a good, standard version of the dish as it is normally served, not the most extreme example that exists — regional versions and individual cooks move them around. They are for comparison between dishes, not measurement.

    On authority. Nothing here is a ruling about what is good. Balance, contrast and restraint are near-universal in dishes that people return to, but the pleasure of a food is not a property of the food alone: it is the food plus your history with it. Use the vocabulary to describe your own taste more precisely, not to overrule it.

    On the acquired tastes. Bitter, funky and burning foods are learned, and the learning is real — repeated exposure measurably shifts what registers as pleasant. But there is no obligation to climb any of these ladders. Preferring mild food is not an unfinished palate.
